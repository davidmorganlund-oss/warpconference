About the day

Washington's first statewide convening for restorative justice, reentry, and justice system innovation.

WARP was built different. We funded participation from rural reentry organizations in counties across Washington and centered tribal voices as program leaders.

Travel, lodging, and access costs were underwritten for organizations that otherwise could not have been in the room.

One day. The whole state in the room.

WARP brought together corrections leaders, tribal justice practitioners, rural nonprofits, behavioral health and housing providers, researchers, policymakers, and people with lived experience for a single day of learning, connection, and policy dialogue at the University of Washington Bothell.

Why this summit

Four reasons WARP matters

Learn

Breakout sessions on reentry, housing, behavioral health, employment, and civic engagement, led by practitioners and researchers from across the state.

Connect

A rare cross-sector space where corrections leaders, tribal practitioners, rural nonprofits, policymakers, and people with lived experience share the same room.

Advance

Policy dialogue tied to live reform questions in Washington that are actionable. Learning with real statewide reach, not just inspiration.

Elevate

Healing-centered, culturally grounded justice models built into every program segment and centered from beginning to end.

Built for broad participation

We make the room reflect the state.

Statewide justice convenings have historically been anchored in Seattle and shaped primarily by urban organizations. WARP is designed from the start to be different.

Travel, lodging, and access costs are covered for organizations across Washington, including rural reentry programs and Tribal Nations, so that being in the room does not depend on what a budget can absorb.

Geography and budget should not determine who gets a seat at a statewide conversation.

For attorneys & legal system practitioners

If you work in the criminal legal system, start here. These are the resources most relevant to clients who are incarcerated, in reentry, or eligible for diversion.

  • RCW 13.40.020 and RCW 13.40.080: Washington's statutory definition of restorative justice and juvenile diversion referrals
  • 2SHB 1391 (2025): diversion may include restorative justice; completed diversions removed from criminal history
  • DOC Victim-Offender Dialogue: survivor-initiated, and it does not affect custody, release, or clemency
  • Collective Justice and Freedom Project: circle and healing programs inside Washington prisons and in reentry
  • Reading: Danielle Sered, Until We Reckon; Barb Toews, The Little Book of Restorative Justice for People in Prison; Shadd Maruna, Making Good

Washington law & policy

Statutes, rules, and reports that reference restorative justice in Washington.

  • RCW 13.40.020Defines restorative justice in the Juvenile Justice Act: victim-sensitive practices that encourage the person who caused harm to take responsibility for repairing it through voluntary communication among the victim, offender, families, and community.
  • RCW 13.40.080Juvenile diversion. A diversion unit may refer a young person to a restorative justice program.
  • 2SHB 1391 (2025)Juvenile diversion reform (Chapter 140, Laws of 2025, effective July 27, 2025). Diversion may include restorative justice, completed diversions are removed from criminal history, and the state now collects statewide diversion data.
  • SHB 1775 (2012)Washington's first statutory definition of restorative justice; allowed completing a restorative process to satisfy a juvenile diversion agreement.
  • RCW 7.75The Court Improvement Act, which authorizes and funds Washington's Dispute Resolution Centers.
  • RCW 7.69.030Crime victims' bill of rights, important context for any survivor-initiated restorative process.
  • WAC 392-400K–12 student discipline rules requiring restorative and proactive supports and limiting suspension and expulsion. Revised rules effective July 11, 2025.
  • DOC Policy 390.350The Department of Corrections policy governing victim-offender dialogue.
  • UW report to the Legislature (2021)Beckett & Herbert, Developing Restorative Justice as a Response to Hate Crime in Washington, recommending a state restorative justice program that funds community-based organizations.

Literature & media

Foundational reading, films, and podcasts on circle processes, restorative practice, and incarceration. Living Justice Press publishes many of the core circle texts.

Circle processes

  • Peacemaking Circles: From Crime to CommunityKay Pranis, Barry Stuart & Mark Wedge (2003)The classic account of sentencing and community circles, drawn from Yukon First Nations practice. Essential for justice system audiences. Living Justice Press →
  • The Little Book of Circle ProcessesKay Pranis (2005)A short, practical introduction to circle structure, talking pieces, and the role of the keeper.
  • Circle Forward: Building a Restorative School CommunityCarolyn Boyes-Watson & Kay Pranis (2015; rev. 2020)Ready-to-use circle plans for schools. Publisher →
  • Heart of HopeCarolyn Boyes-Watson & Kay Pranis (2010)A circle curriculum for emotional literacy and healing with young people.
  • Peacemaking Circles and Urban YouthCarolyn Boyes-Watson (2008)Circles in youth justice and community settings.
  • Calling the Circle & The Circle WayChristina Baldwin (1994); Christina Baldwin & Ann Linnea (2010)Circle practice for groups and organizations.
  • Returning to the TeachingsRupert Ross (1996)Indigenous approaches to justice, including Hollow Water's Community Holistic Circle Healing.
  • Navajo Nation Peacemaking: Living Traditional JusticeMarianne O. Nielsen & James W. Zion, eds. (2005)Traditional peacemaking as practiced within a tribal justice system.
  • Origins of CirclesLiving Justice Press (free)A short primer on the Indigenous roots of circle practice. Read online →
  • Reentry circlesLorenn Walker & Leslie Greening, Federal Probation (2010)The Hawaiʻi Huikahi model, the most-cited approach to reentry planning circles.

Restorative justice foundations

  • Changing LensesHoward Zehr (1990; 25th anniv. ed. 2015)The founding argument for restorative justice as a different way of seeing crime and justice.
  • The Little Book of Restorative JusticeHoward Zehr (2002; rev. 2015)The best one-hour primer, for any setting.
  • Until We ReckonDanielle Sered (2019)The case for restorative justice in serious violent crime, from the founder of Common Justice.
  • The Little Book of Race and Restorative Justicefania e. davis (2019)Connects restorative justice to racial justice, from the founder of RJOY.
  • Restorative Justice DialogueMark Umbreit & Marilyn Peterson Armour (2010)Victim-offender dialogue, including in cases of severe violence.
  • Conflicts as PropertyNils Christie, British Journal of Criminology (1977)The classic essay arguing that conflicts belong to the people and communities closest to them, and that professionalized justice "stole" them. The root of the idea of direct conflict ownership.
  • Crime, Shame and ReintegrationJohn Braithwaite (1989)The theory of reintegrative shaming that underpins much restorative work.

Prison, reentry & community accountability

  • The Little Book of Restorative Justice for People in PrisonBarb Toews (2006)Written for incarcerated readers, and useful for anyone working inside.
  • Making Good: How Ex-Convicts Reform and Rebuild Their LivesShadd Maruna (2001)Desistance research that grounds restorative reentry.
  • We Do This 'Til We Free UsMariame Kaba (2021)Essays on abolition, transformative justice, and community accountability.
  • Fumbling Towards RepairMariame Kaba & Shira Hassan (2019)A workbook for community accountability facilitators.
  • We Will Not Cancel Usadrienne maree brown (2020)Accountability and repair inside movements and communities.

Research & evidence

The evidence is real, and it's mixed. Restorative conferencing reliably improves outcomes for survivors and modestly reduces reoffending; Washington-specific cost-benefit results are more cautious. We share both so you can make the case honestly.

Systematic review

Campbell Collaboration (2013)

Strang, Sherman et al. reviewed ten randomized studies of face-to-face conferencing: modestly less repeat offending, larger effects for violent than property crime, and consistently higher victim satisfaction.

campbellcollaboration.org →
Randomized trial · schools

RAND Pittsburgh study (2018)

Across 44 schools, restorative practices reduced suspensions and narrowed racial gaps, and teachers reported better climate. It found no academic gains, and middle school achievement dipped.

rand.org →
Washington

WSIPP benefit-cost results

Juvenile conferencing and victim-offender mediation have a 78% chance that benefits exceed costs, mostly because they cost less than court. Adult conferencing and school programs score lower.

Juvenile → Adult → Schools →

Washington · prisons

Freedom Project study (2014)

Suarez et al. in SAGE Open found a 21% return-to-prison rate among 885 Freedom Project participants, compared with 37% statewide. The design is correlational, so it can't rule out who chose to participate.
